# Coal and Petroleum: Formation, Distribution, and Simulation Rules from Burial and Thermal History

Scope: coal (peat to graphite) and petroleum (oil, gas, oil sands, oil shale), written to support rule-based simulation on a coarse plate-tectonic world grid (about 16 km cells) with per-cell stratigraphic columns, burial/temperature histories, climate, sea level, and a life timeline.

Notation: Ro = vitrinite reflectance (%); TOC = total organic carbon (wt%); HI = hydrogen index (mg HC/g TOC); TTI = Lopatin/Waples time-temperature index; Ma = million years ago; Myr = million years.

Source-quality note: many items come from abstracts, wiki pages, or search-engine summaries of papers that I could not read in full. Where a number is unverified, or where its link to a specific URL is uncertain, it is flagged. Values marked "unverified (general knowledge)" come from standard textbook knowledge that I could not confirm with a source in this session. The report writer should treat them as design defaults, not as cited facts.

## 1. Coal: peat-forming environments, climate, land plants, compaction, rank, seams, cyclothems, coalbed methane

### Takeaway
Coal needs four things together. (1) Peat-forming land plants: none before about 400 Ma (Early-Middle Devonian), and a coal gap of about 10 Myr after the end-Permian extinction. (2) A water table that stays at or above the surface all year, meaning ever-wet climate and precipitation greater than evaporation. This can happen at tropical or high latitudes. (3) Subsidence that matches peat growth, with little clastic input, in coastal plains, deltas, alluvial floodplains, and foreland or intermontane basins. (4) Burial under sediment. Rank then depends mainly on maximum temperature: sub-bituminous about 35-80 °C, anthracite at least about 180-245 °C. Peat compacts about 2-4:1 to lignite and about 10-20:1 to bituminous coal.

### Cited Findings

**Land plants and the timeline**
- The oldest coals occur in Devonian deposits. The earliest coaly rocks with plant remains are Pragian-Emsian (Early Devonian, New Brunswick, Canada). Middle Devonian coals are known from Germany, Kazakhstan, China, Illinois, and elsewhere — [Bushnev et al. 2024](https://pureportal.spbu.ru/files/116922033/Bushnev_et_al._2024.pdf)
- Mid and Late Devonian palaeoequatorial humic (vitrinite-rich) coals are dominated by lycopsid spores (associated with *Protolepidodendropsis*). The progymnosperm *Archaeopteris* was abundant but did not form the coal. There is some evidence for a Tournaisian (earliest Carboniferous) coal gap — [Marshall, Tel'nova & Berry, via CyberLeninka](https://cyberleninka.ru/article/n/devonian-and-early-carboniferous-coals-and-the-evolution-of-wetlands)
- Early coals are thin. West Virginia's oldest coal (Upper Devonian Hampshire Fm) occurs in impure beds generally only a few centimetres thick — [e-WV Encyclopedia](https://wvencyclopedia.org/entries/1286)
- Rank and age: most anthracite and bituminous coal is in Carboniferous strata (299-359 Ma). Cretaceous coals are generally high- to medium-volatile bituminous. Cenozoic coals are mostly sub-bituminous and lignite — [Britannica](https://britannica.com/technology/coal-mining/Coal-deposits)
- Share of world coal deposits by period: Permian about 29%, Carboniferous 20%, Tertiary 21%, Jurassic 15%, Cretaceous 15%, with small Devonian and Triassic deposits — [AAPG Wiki (revision page)](https://wiki.aapg.org/Special:MobileDiff/15349). This conflicts with [Wikipedia: Coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal), which claims "90% of all coal beds were deposited in the Carboniferous and Permian". The AAPG/McCabe figures are more conservative and better supported.
- By coal-bearing strata, not tonnage: Late Carboniferous strata are 21% of the world's coal-bearing strata. Global coal accumulation rates appear "relatively constant since the end of the Carboniferous", except the Triassic, which has only 1.75% of coal-bearing strata — [McCabe abstract, AAPG/Search and Discovery](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2012/90155ice/abstracts/mc.htm)

**The coal gap (Early Triassic)**
- Retallack et al. (1996): Early Triassic coals are unknown, and Middle Triassic coals are rare and thin. They attribute this to extinction of peat-forming plants at the Permian-Triassic boundary, followed by a hiatus of about 10 Myr until new peat-forming plants tolerated acidic, dysaerobic wetlands. They rejected tectonic or climatic explanations, because coal-typical facies, sea-level changes, and paleosols are still present in the Early Triassic — [OSTI record of Retallack, Veevers & Morante 1996, GSA Bulletin](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/420910)
- Refinement: no coal anywhere until the Middle Triassic (about 243 Ma). Permian levels of plant diversity and peat thickness did not recover until the Late Triassic (about 230 Ma) — [Retallack group paper, NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10158563) (attribution of these exact numbers to this URL comes from a search summary).
- Middle Triassic Nymboida Coal Measures (Australia) record peat-forming communities re-established about 10 Myr after the end-Permian extinction — [Proc. Linnean Soc. NSW](https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/LIN/article/view/16680/14092)
- Northern Bowen Basin: a long-lived (about 9 Myr) cold-climate peat-mire ecosystem collapsed at the P-T boundary, when about 95% of peat-producing plants went extinct — [JCU Research Online](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/13657/)

**Lignin, fungi, and the Carboniferous peak (debate)**
- Lag hypothesis: plants evolved lignin about 360 Ma. Lignin-degrading fungi appeared about 300 Ma, though one phylogenetic estimate gives about 200 Ma. The gap between them let wood accumulate — [Wikipedia: Coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal)
- Nelsen, DiMichele, Peters & Boyce (2016, PNAS) reject the lag hypothesis. Their arguments: lignin degradation was probably never limited to Agaricomycetes. Many Carboniferous coals are mostly lignin-poor lycopsid bark. Coal accumulation rates did not change as floras shifted. High coal accumulation continues wherever conditions allow. They attribute the Carboniferous peak to ever-wet tropical conditions plus extensive depositional systems (subsiding basins) during Pangea assembly — [Smithsonian Research Online](https://repository.si.edu/items/52e46b6c-12ec-44d5-8ce6-160781d13956); [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/2016/01/22/coal-formation-pangea-012216/)
- Popular coverage presented the paper as moving attention back to geology and climate, while noting the debate continues — [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/getting-to-the-root-of-how-earths-massive-coal-seams-formed)
- Other proposed Carboniferous factors: atmospheric O2 above 30%, high CO2, lycopsid forests with determinate growth, the equatorial Central Pangean Mountains giving year-round rain with no monsoon dry season, and glacial lowstands that exposed shelves and expanded deltas — [Wikipedia: Coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal) (secondary; the O2 value is contested in the literature).

**Climate requirements**
- Central Appalachian Basin: in the latest Mississippian to earliest Pennsylvanian, climate shifted from dry-seasonal tropical to ever-wet (equable) tropical, and stayed so into the Middle Pennsylvanian. Many peats were probably domed raised bogs with weakly buffered, acidic, nutrient-poor water, which gave low-ash, low-sulfur coal. When evaporation periodically exceeded rainfall later, peats became planar and the coals more variable in ash and sulfur — [Cecil et al. 1985, USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70012717)
- Cecil argues climate, not relative sea level, was the primary control on peat formation. Sea level matters more for burial and preservation of peat — [Cecil abstract, AAPG 2000](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2000/annual/abstracts/0107.htm)
- Alternative view: continent-wide peat formation was triggered by a climate change from seasonally dry to ever-wet together with a coeval sea-level rise that raised the water table. "Commercial-grade, high quality coal beds are prima facie evidence of humid climates" — [Falcon-Lang, DiMichele et al. 2010, Palaios](https://repository.si.edu/bitstream/handle/10088/15974/paleo_Falcon_Lang_DiMichele_CoalForestRefugia_Palaios_2010.pdf)
- Coal also formed at high latitude in cold climates: Permian Gondwanan (glossopterid) peat mires of the Bowen and Sydney basins, about 9 Myr long-lived, "cold climate" — [JCU Research Online](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/13657/). Peat forms in low-lying wetlands where plant matter is protected from oxidation by water or mud — [Wikipedia: Coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal)

**Peat accumulation rates and thickness (modern analogues)**
- 28 European (temperate/boreal) peatlands, last 2,000 years: apparent accumulation 0.05-4.5 mm/yr, mean about 1.2 mm/yr. Climate and hydrology control the rate — [Exeter ORE](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/140884)
- Tropical peat domes, Central Sumatra: peat about 4 m thick at the dome margin and more than 9 m at the centre — [Progress in Earth and Planetary Science 2019](https://progearthplanetsci.springeropen.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s40645-019-0288-8)
- Kalimantan: early Holocene rates above 2 mm/yr (9.5-8.6 ka), then 0.15-0.38 mm/yr. A 9.5 m core gave a long-term carbon accumulation of 56 g C/m²/yr. Southern Borneo modelling shows apparent accumulation falling from about 0.5 to about 0.1 mm/yr under gradual drying — [UNH Scholars](https://scholars.unh.edu/earthsci_facpub/381); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/132969) (these numbers come from a search summary, and which number belongs to which of these two papers is not certain).

**Peat-to-coal compaction**
- Published peat:coal ratios range from about 1.1:1 to 60:1. For lignite the most reliable peat:lignite ratio is 2:1 to 4:1 — [Widera, Annales Societatis Geologorum Poloniae / Geological Quarterly review](https://geojournals.pgi.gov.pl/agp/article/view/24833)
- Pennsylvanian bituminous coals: peat:coal ratios are "commonly interpreted as 10:1 or more" — [Univ. Illinois, "In support of early peat-to-coal compaction"](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/in-support-of-early-peat-to-coal-compaction-evidence-from-pennsyl/)
- Oligotrophic peat to medium-volatile bituminous coal: about 20:1, and "most bituminous coal mined today" fits about 20:1 — [GSA 2001 abstract](https://gsa.confex.com/gsa/2001AM/webprogram/Paper26215.html)
- A Cretaceous bituminous coal in central Utah gave a peat:coal thickness ratio of about 11:1 — [USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70012373)

**Rank vs temperature, depth, and Ro**
- Temperature "is much more important than either pressure or time of burial". Sub-bituminous coal forms at about 35-80 °C. Anthracite needs "at least 180 to 245 °C" — [Wikipedia: Coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal)
- Sub-bituminous coal: Ro generally 0.4-0.5%, calorific value 8,300-11,500 Btu/lb. Sub-bituminous A overlaps with high-volatile C bituminous in both Ro and calorific value. Above sub-bituminous B, changes are mainly thermal rather than biological — [Kentucky Geological Survey](https://www.uky.edu/KGS/coal/coal-sub.php)
- Published Ro values for rank-class boundaries disagree, mainly within high-volatile bituminous — [USGS OF 79-725](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1979/0725/report.pdf)
- Anthracite to meta-anthracite with Ro about 3.9-5.2% corresponds to peak temperatures of about 290-360 °C (Swiss Carboniferous) — [Swiss Journal of Geosciences 2008](https://sjg.springeropen.com/articles/10.1007/s00015-008-1298-5)
- Graphitization marks the end of coalification (d002 about 0.334 nm). In the lab, anthracite needs about 2000 °C or more to graphitize, or as low as about 600 °C under high confining pressure. Natural graphitization of coal therefore needs metamorphic conditions (pressure plus heat), not normal basin burial — [Frontiers in Earth Science 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/earth-science/articles/10.3389/feart.2023.1235457/full)
- Ro as a maximum-temperature geothermometer: Barker and Pawlewicz proposed that time is nearly irrelevant, because Ro equilibrates at peak temperature within a few years. Kinetic models such as EASY%Ro instead include time — [AAPG Explorer (Burnham)](https://www.aapg.org/news-and-media/details/explorer/articleid/60439/modernizing-vitrinite-reflectance-models-for-paleothermal-history-calibration)
- Barker (1983), geothermal systems: Rm = 0.435·exp(0.00683·T), with T in °C — [USGS publication record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70011965) (link between this equation and this record comes from a search summary).

**Coal environments, seam geometry, tectonic setting**
- Powder River Basin (intermontane, Laramide): the Wyodak-Anderson and Felix beds are anomalously thick (more than 10 m). They formed as fluvial peat in restricted floodplain pockets between contemporaneous anastomosed channels — [USGS, Warwick & Stanton 1988](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/depositional-models-two-tertiary-coal-bearing-sequences-powder-river-basin-wyoming-usa)
- Toward the west and north, the Wyodak zone thins and splits into multiple discontinuous beds, and sandstone intervals thicken up to tenfold. This is attributed to thrust loading from the Bighorn uplift subsiding the basin axis — [USGS 2010](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/after-a-century-revised-paleogene-coal-stratigraphy-correlation-and-deposition-powder). Structural lineaments line up with abrupt coal-thickness changes and no-coal channel zones — [Zilinski & Goldstein 2005](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/documents/abstracts/2005rocky/RMzil.htm)

**Cyclothems and sea level**
- Black Warrior Basin (Alabama, Pennsylvanian foreland): cyclothems last at most about 0.4 Myr (long eccentricity). Each holds four 5th-order parasequences, and coal clusters within them. Widespread coal beds up to 3 m thick occur in the 2nd and 3rd parasequences — [Pashin 2004 abstract](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2004/annual/abstracts/Pashin02.htm)
- Earlier work assumed about 400 kyr cycles throughout. Radiometric dating of central Appalachian coals indicates that parts of the Early and Middle Pennsylvanian were dominated by 100 kyr (short-eccentricity) cycles — [AAPG 2016 abstract, "Changing duration of Carboniferous cyclothems"](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2016/90258es/abstracts/9.20.html)
- A spectral study gives about 256 m of section for about 395 kyr and about 59 m for about 95 kyr. It notes the glacio-eustatic interpretation "lacks unambiguous quantitative support" and competes with precipitation-driven climate models — [Utrecht University](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/revealing-the-hidden-milankovitch-record-from-pennsylvanian-cyclo/)

**Coalbed methane (CBM)**
- Higher-rank coals generate more methane and less CO2. Coal rank, gas content, and present-day depth control CBM potential, and thermally mature coals at shallow depth are preferred targets — [Bureau of Economic Geology 1992 abstract](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/1992/annual/abstracts/0116.htm)
- Main-stage thermogenic methane starts at Ro about 0.8-1.0% (high-volatile A bituminous). Early thermogenic gas forms below Ro 0.8%. Secondary biogenic gas can form at any rank after uplift lets meteoric water reach the coal. High-rank coals can exceed 800 scf/ton — [Kansas Geological Survey poster](https://www.kgs.ku.edu/PRS/publication/2004/AAPG/Coalbed/AAPG04POSTER%20panel%20C.pdf)
- Wilcox lignites (Texas): below 30 scf/t in shallow (<600 m) coals and 150-400 scf/t in deep (>1,200 m) coals — [Ayers 2005 abstract](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2005/annual/abstracts/ayers.htm). Thermally immature Powder River coals (Ro <0.5%) hold less than 75 scf/ton — [USGS OF 96-735](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1996/of96-735/cbm_comp.htm) (attribution from a search summary).

### Inferences
- **Peat-accumulation rule per cell per time step (design suggestion).** Peat accumulates when all of these hold:
  - (a) Peat-forming land plants exist. On Earth that is about 400 Ma for thin coals and about 360 Ma for widespread coals. It is turned off for about 10 Myr after a major terrestrial extinction, and thickness returns to normal only about 20 Myr after.
  - (b) The cell is a low-gradient, near-sea-level or waterlogged depositional setting: delta plain, coastal plain, alluvial floodplain, lake margin, or interdune or interfan pockets in intermontane or foreland basins.
  - (c) Annual precipitation exceeds evapotranspiration with no long dry season. Use a "wet months" or P-E index rather than temperature. Tropical ever-wet and cool-temperate or boreal ever-wet both qualify, but tropical mires grow faster.
  - (d) Clastic input is low, so the cell is not on an active channel belt or delta front.
  - (e) Accommodation (subsidence plus sea-level rise) roughly equals peat growth. Too slow and peat oxidizes; too fast and the mire drowns into lake or marine mud.
- **Peat growth rate.** Use about 0.5-2 mm/yr of peat (0.5-2 m per kyr) during favourable intervals. One metre of bituminous coal at about 10-20:1 compaction is about 10-20 m of peat. At about 1 mm/yr that is about 10-20 kyr, which fits within a single 100-400 kyr cyclothem. A cell time step of about 1 Myr is too coarse to resolve individual seams. Emit stochastic "seam packages" per step instead: the number of seams is about step length divided by cycle length (100 or 400 kyr) times the fraction of cycles with peat. Seam thickness is about 0.3-3 m, with rare 10-30 m seams in rapidly subsiding intermontane or foreland basins (Powder River type).
- **Compaction factor by rank.** Peat to lignite about 3:1. Peat to sub-bituminous about 5-7:1 (interpolated, unverified). Peat to bituminous about 10-20:1. Peat to anthracite about 20:1 or more (unverified).
- **Rank from maximum temperature (Tmax), as default thresholds.**
  - Peat: Tmax below about 35 °C (shallow, under about 1 km).
  - Lignite: below about 50 °C. This boundary is interpolated and unverified. The Wikipedia range for sub-bituminous is 35-80 °C, which overlaps lignite.
  - Sub-bituminous: about 50-80 °C.
  - Bituminous: about 80-180 °C (high-volatile below about 140 °C, medium and low volatile above).
  - Anthracite: about 180-300 °C.
  - Meta-anthracite: about 300-360 °C.
  - Graphite: only where the column has been metamorphosed (greenschist or higher, roughly 400 °C or more plus deformation). This last threshold is unverified (general knowledge). Map graphite to collision-zone metamorphism, not basin burial.
  - Anthracite on Earth is typically tied to fold-thrust belts or deep foreland burial (Pennsylvania anthracite fields). This is consistent with the 180-245 °C requirement but not directly sourced here.
- **Ro-temperature conversion (commonly quoted; unverified in this session).** Barker & Pawlewicz (1994) burial-heating relation: Tpeak(°C) = (ln Ro + 1.68) / 0.0124. This gives Ro 0.6% at about 94 °C, 1.3% at about 157 °C, 2.0% at about 191 °C, and 4.0% at about 247 °C. These values agree with the cited anthracite threshold of about 180-245 °C. Verify the coefficients before relying on them.
- **Approximate Ro rank boundaries (unverified, general knowledge after ASTM D388).** Lignite below about 0.4%. Sub-bituminous 0.4-0.5% (cited above). High-volatile bituminous about 0.5-1.1%. Medium-volatile about 1.1-1.5%. Low-volatile about 1.5-2.0%. Semi-anthracite about 2.0-2.5%. Anthracite above about 2.5% (meta-anthracite above about 4-5%).
- **TFC mapping.** TFC "lignite" corresponds to peat-lignite-sub-bituminous ranks (Tmax below about 80 °C). TFC "bituminous coal" corresponds to the bituminous band. Anthracite would map to vanilla coal or a custom block. Low-rank Cenozoic basins should mostly yield lignite. Old, deeply buried Carboniferous-equivalent basins should yield bituminous or anthracite.
- **CBM rule.** Any bituminous or higher seam with Ro at least about 0.8% gets a methane charge, scaled with rank and current depth. Lower-rank seams get biogenic gas only if the basin has been uplifted or flushed by meteoric water.
- **Coal basins in tectonic terms.** Coal is favoured where long-lived subsidence meets wet climate: foreland basins (flexural subsidence ahead of thrust belts), intermontane or broken-foreland basins (Laramide type), rift and post-rift coastal plains, and cratonic shelves with cyclothems. Passive margins host Cenozoic lignite on coastal plains, such as the Gulf Coast Wilcox.

### Gaps
- No authoritative ASTM D388 or ISO 11760 Ro boundary table was retrieved. The Ro rank cutoffs above are general knowledge.
- No direct source for peat-to-sub-bituminous or peat-to-anthracite compaction ratios.
- No quantitative data on lateral extent of individual seams (for example the Herrin or Pittsburgh coals, often quoted as tens of thousands of km²) or on overall coal-basin areas.
- The temperature at which natural graphite forms from coal (metamorphic grade) was not sourced.
- No sourced rule for the minimum precipitation or maximum dry-season length that still allows peat. Cecil's "ever-wet" is qualitative.
- The Nelsen et al. vs lignin-lag debate continues. No 2024-2026 consensus statement was found.

## 2. Petroleum system elements and processes (Magoon & Dow)

### Takeaway
A petroleum accumulation needs, in the right order: an organic-rich source rock (TOC at least about 1-2%, kerogen Type I, II or III by environment), burial into the oil window (about 60-150 °C, Ro about 0.6-1.3%) or gas window (about 150-200 °C or more, Ro above 1.3%), expulsion and buoyant updip migration through carrier beds or faults, a porous reservoir under a seal (shale or evaporite), and a trap that formed before the "critical moment" of migration. Afterwards the accumulation must survive. Below about 80 °C it is biodegraded to heavy oil or bitumen, which forms oil sands at the surface. Immature organic-rich rock is "oil shale".

### Cited Findings

**System definition and timing**
- Generation-accumulation efficiency (GAE) = trapped in-place petroleum divided by petroleum generated by the active source-rock pod, in percent — [AAPG Wiki: Petroleum systems](https://wiki.aapg.org/Petroleum_systems)
- Magoon & Dow's "critical moment" is the time that best represents generation, migration, and accumulation of most hydrocarbons in a system. Traps must exist by then — [AAPG Wiki (revision)](https://wiki.aapg.org/Special:MobileDiff/18933)
- Example: a Middle Jurassic source generated about 1.34×10¹² kg, with about 5.3×10¹¹ kg in discovered traps. The search summary gave this as about 4%, but the quoted figures actually give about 40%, so this example needs checking before use. The most prolific system is not necessarily the most efficient — [AAPG Methods in Exploration (Datapages)](https://archives.datapages.com/data/specpubs/methodo2/data/a077/a077/0001/0350/0359.htm); [same series](https://archives.datapages.com/data/specpubs/methodo2/data/a077/a077/0001/0300/0329.htm)
- End members: an "open" system (Dow 1974), where all expelled oil migrates, pressures are near hydrostatic, and trapping efficiency is low; and a "closed" system (Meissner 1978), with no migration, overpressure, and oil retained in the source. The latter is the basis of shale oil — [North Dakota GS GI-178](https://www.dmr.nd.gov/ndgs/documents/Publication_List/pdf/GEOINV/GI178/GI-178.pdf)

**Source rocks: TOC and kerogen**
- TOC quantity categories (Peters et al. 2005, after Tissot & Welte): poor <0.5, fair 0.5-1, good 1-2, very good 2-4, excellent >4 wt%. A 2023 revision (K. Peters, personal communication) raises these to poor <1, fair 1-2, good 2-3, very good 3-4, excellent >4 — [IGI Ltd](https://igiltd.com/assets/42.-Green_Total-Organic-Carbon.pdf)
- HI thresholds (Peters & Cassa 1994): below 200 is gas-prone, 200-300 mixed, above 300 oil-prone — [GEUS Bulletin](https://geusbulletin.org/index.php/geusb/article/download/4989/10659/31750). Type II kerogen HI is about 300-600 — [AAPG Wiki (revision)](https://wiki.aapg.org/Special:MobileDiff/21702)
- Kerogen types — [Wikipedia: Kerogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerogen)

| Type | Source and setting | H/C | Products |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Lacustrine algae in anoxic lakes (rarely marine) | >1.25 | Highest oil yield |
| II | Marine plankton in reducing marine settings | <1.25 | Oil plus gas |
| II-S | Sulfur-rich Type II | — | Oil plus gas |
| III | Land plants (lignin, cellulose); coal is mostly this type | <1 | Mainly gas |
| IV | Inert | <0.5 | No hydrocarbons |

- Arabian example: Jurassic and Cretaceous intrashelf-basin source rocks (Kazhdumi, Sargelu, Tuwaiq Mountain shales and marls) charge carbonate reservoirs — [USGS Fact Sheet 2024-3048](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/fs20243048/full). Most giant Asmari (Oligo-Miocene) and Bangestan (Upper Cretaceous) oil comes from the Albian Kazhdumi Fm — [Burwood 1984, AAPG](https://archives.datapages.com/data/specpubs/geochem1/data/a030/a030/0001/0200/0206a.htm)

**Maturation windows**
- Oil window about 50-150 °C. Gas window about 150-200 °C. Both depend on heating rate — [Wikipedia: Kerogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerogen)
- Ro schemes vary:
  - Paleochem/Sodir: oil threshold 0.6%, oil floor 1.3%, condensate floor about 2.0%, gas floor 3.2% — [Sodir factpages report](https://factpages.sodir.no/pbl/geochemical_pdfs/128_2.pdf)
  - Weatherford: oil 0.6-1.1%, wet gas/condensate 1.1-1.4%, dry gas 1.4% to about 3-4% — [NWT well report](https://www.orogo.gov.nt.ca/system/files/well-documents/2013%20-%20Report%20-%20Appendix%20I%20-%20Ro%20Report.pdf)
  - BGS: oil onset 0.5-0.6%, end 0.85-1.1%. Gas window from 1.0-1.3% to about 3.0% — [BGS OR/14/055](https://earthwise.bgs.ac.uk/index.php/OR/14/055_Technical_information)
- For humic coals and Type III kerogen the oil window is wider and shifted: Ro 0.85-1.8% — [Petersen 2002, J. Petroleum Geology](https://jpg.co.uk/ab_oct_02_2.htm)
- Lopatin TTI thresholds: onset of oil 15, peak 75, end of oil generation 160. Oil preservation limits are about 500 (40° API) and about 1,000 (50° API). Wet gas preservation limit about 1,500. Dry gas above 65,000 — [IPA, NW Java basin](https://www.ipa.or.id/en/publications/a-hydrocarbon-generation-analysis-in-northwest-java-basin-using-lopatin-s-method); calibration list via [GSC Open File 1178](https://download-telecharger.services.geo.ca/pub/nrcan_rncan/publications/STPublications_PublicationsST/130/130008/of_1178.pdf) (attribution of the preservation values to this file comes from a search summary).
- TTI rate factor doubles every 10 °C: 2^-10 for 0-10 °C up to 2^-2 for 80-90 °C, which implies 2^0 = 1 for 100-110 °C. TTI = Σ (factor × Myr spent in each interval) — [Geochemical Transactions, Nankai table](https://geochemicaltransactions.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12932-014-0015-8/tables/3)
- Critics say TTI has no theoretical basis compared with kinetic models, while others note it agrees well with measured maturity parameters — [IPA paper summary](https://www.ipa.or.id/en/publications/a-hydrocarbon-generation-analysis-in-northwest-java-basin-using-lopatin-s-method)
- EASY%Ro (Sweeney & Burnham 1990, AAPG Bull. 74:1559-1570) uses a distribution of activation energies and is "perhaps the most widely used model" for calibrating thermal histories. Revised versions exist that raise Ro faster at high grade — [GEO ExPro](https://geoexpro.com/a-cool-vitrinite-reflectance-model/); [Basin Research (Earthdoc)](https://www.earthdoc.org/content/journals/10.1111/bre.12317); [AAPG Explorer](https://www.aapg.org/news-and-media/details/explorer/articleid/60439/modernizing-vitrinite-reflectance-models-for-paleothermal-history-calibration)
- Pepper & Corvi (1995, Mar. Pet. Geol. 12:291-319) give "standard" organofacies kinetics (A-F) that industry uses when measured kinetics are unavailable. A typical geological heating rate used for comparison is 3 °C/Myr — [Warwick repository](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/77034/); [AAPG Bulletin 2019 figure](https://archives.datapages.com/data/bulletns/2019/01jan/BLTN17249/images/fg5.htm); [Chevron abstract](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2012/90142ace/abstracts/curi.htm)

**Reservoir porosity vs burial**
- Sandstone intergranular volume falls quickly from about 40-42% at the surface to about 28% at about 1.5 km (5,000 ft), then slowly to a stable framework of about 26% — [Szabo & Paxton abstract](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/1991/annual/abstracts/0678b.htm)
- Experiments at 20-25 MPa effective stress (about 2-2.5 km): coarse sand porosity falls from about 43% to about 30%, while fine sand keeps nearly 40% — [Bjørlykke et al. 2001](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2001/annual/abstracts/0077.htm)
- Below about 2 km and about 75 °C, quartz cementation dominates porosity loss. Models with mechanical compaction only overestimate deep porosity — [NTNU Open](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2440553?show=full)
- Zagros and Persian Gulf: about 85% of oil reserves and more than 90% of gas and condensate are in carbonate reservoirs — [GEO ExPro: Persia](https://geoexpro.com/persia-land-of-black-gold/)

**Seals, traps, migration**
- Ghawar: an Upper Arab-D anhydrite seals a 1,300 ft (about 400 m) oil column — [CSEG Recorder](https://csegrecorder.com/articles/view/what-you-know-about-the-ghawar-oil-field-saudi-arabia)
- Giant fields: about 95% of reserves are in structure-related traps, and about three-quarters of those are anticlines. Continental margins and offshore areas have more combination and flowage (salt) traps — [SEAPEX abstract (undated)](https://archives.datapages.com/data/southeast-asia-petroleum-exploration-society/data/001/001001/44_seapex0010044.htm)
- Gulf of Mexico shelf: structural traps are 93% of traps and 94% of oil reserves; stratigraphic traps 7% and 6%. Flank traps on salt or shale diapirs are 14% of traps — [Oil & Gas Journal](https://ogj.com/general-interest/companies/article/17228764/trap-types-can-be-instructive-in-gulf-of-mexico-shelf-search)
- Deep accumulations (below about 4.6 km): 73.8% structural, 21.9% combination, 4.4% stratigraphic. Salt-bearing deep basins look more prospective, possibly because salt cools the sediments beneath it — [Oil & Gas Journal, deep reservoirs](https://www.ogj.com/exploration-development/reserves/article/17240568/more-attention-recommended-for-global-deep-reservoirs)
- Persian Gulf: "as many as 60%" of traps may be formed by evaporite (Hormuz salt) diapirism (contested). Salt-withdrawal synclines act as kitchens — [GEO ExPro](https://geoexpro.com/persia-land-of-black-gold/); [Zagros FTB, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagros_fold_and_thrust_belt)
- Numerical benchmarks (Darcy simulator vs invasion percolation) both show migrating oil focusing to anticline crests, then leaking into the top seal once the oil column becomes buoyant enough — [Permedia/MPATH paper](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/pdf/2002/hedberg_australia/images/ndx_boettcher.pdf)

**Biodegradation, oil sands, oil shale**
- Below about 80 °C, reservoir oil is commonly biodegraded over geological time by mostly anaerobic, methanogenic microbes into denser heavy oil. Methane is often a by-product — [Head, Jones & Larter 2003, Nature (RePEc)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v426y2003i6964d10.1038_nature02134.html)
- Palaeopasteurization: reservoirs once heated to about 80-90 °C are sterilized, and stay undegraded even after later uplift into cooler conditions — [Wilhelms et al. 2001, Nature (RePEc)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v411y2001i6841d10.1038_35082535.html)
- Athabasca: oil migrated into the Lower Cretaceous McMurray sands after deposition, mainly from a Cretaceous source, possibly with some early Devonian input — [CSPG Bulletin review](https://archives.datapages.com/data/cspg/data/014/014001/0145.htm). One basin model attributes more than 54% of Athabasca and Peace River oil to the Devonian Gordondale and about 15% to the Exshaw. Sources were in the early or main oil window by about 60 Ma, before the Laramide orogeny. Trapping was inefficient until lateral stratigraphic seals developed — [Berbesi et al. 2012, AAPG Bulletin](https://archives.datapages.com/data/bulletns/2012/07jul/BLTN11064/BLTN11064.HTM); [USGS basin-model study](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70005736)
- Shallow burial plus early oil emplacement preserved McMurray porosity. Later biodegradation made the oil into bitumen — [Leckie et al., GCAGS](https://archives.datapages.com/data/gcags/data/058/leckie.htm). Bitumen-water contacts are effectively "frozen" because the viscous bitumen's density is close to water's — [Ranger & Pemberton 1992](https://searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/1992/annual/abstracts/0108c.htm). The original trap was destroyed by uplift, flexure, and erosion — [WOU case study](https://people.wou.edu/~taylors/es486_petro/case_studies/Flury_ES486_Case_Study_Canadian_Tar_Sands.pdf)
- Oil shale: fine-grained rock with kerogen insoluble in solvents that yields oil when heated (retorted). It is effectively an immature source rock with organic carbon but no free oil or gas — [AAPG Wiki: Oil shale](https://wiki.aapg.org/Oil_shale); [Crain's Petrophysics](https://spec2000.net/17-specshoil.htm)
- USGS threshold: at least 4 US gal/ton (17 L/t) by Fischer assay. Green River Fm (lacustrine lamosite, Colorado, Utah, Wyoming) yields up to about 100 gal/ton (417 L/t), about 1.8 trillion bbl in place. Marine oil shales are a separate class derived from marine phytoplankton — [USGS OF 87-628](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1987/0628/report.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Source-rock deposition rule.** Make a cell's layer "source rock" when organic productivity is high and bottom water is anoxic.
  - Type I: stratified lakes in rifts, intermontane or foreland lakes, under warm climate. The Green River is the Earth analogue.
  - Type II: restricted marine basins, intrashelf basins, silled rift seas, upwelling margins, and global anoxic events (Jurassic-Cretaceous on Earth). Requires marine life present.
  - Type II-S: carbonate-evaporite settings (unverified, general knowledge).
  - Type III: deltaic and coastal-plain mudstones and coals. Requires land plants.
  - Assign TOC by drawing from about 1-10% (excellent above 4%). Set HI by type: I about 600-900 (unverified), II about 300-600, III below about 200.
- **Maturity tiers for game logic.**
  - Immature: Ro below 0.6%. Rich immature rock becomes oil shale if TOC is high and it is Type I or II.
  - Oil window: 0.6-1.3%.
  - Wet gas/condensate: 1.3-2.0%.
  - Dry gas: 2.0-3.5%.
  - Overmature: above about 3.5-4%, no further generation, and existing oil is cracked.
  - For Type III kerogen, shift the oil window to about 0.85-1.8%, or simply mark it as mostly gas.
- **Biodegradation rule.** For each accumulation, if reservoir temperature stays below 80 °C, has a water leg, and was never heated above about 85 °C, degrade API gravity over time. Light → medium → heavy → bitumen over tens of Myr (rate unverified). At or near the surface (exhumed reservoir), convert to "oil sand" or "tar sand" blocks, and spawn tar seeps where an accumulation's seal is breached by erosion or faults.
- **Oil sands.** Oil sands require large volumes of oil, a long updip migration to the shallow basin edge in a foreland basin, a shallow reservoir, and late uplift and erosion. The Athabasca analogue is foreland-basin oil migrating up to the eastern cratonic edge.

### Gaps
- Actual Pepper & Corvi activation energies and frequency factors were not retrieved. Single-E "game kinetics" therefore need calibration against the TTI thresholds (see Section 4).
- No sourced quantitative capillary seal capacity (maximum column height) for shale vs salt seals. The Ghawar 400 m column under anhydrite is a single extreme example.
- Expulsion thresholds (for example, the fraction of generated oil retained in source rock before expulsion) were not found.
- Biodegradation rates over time (Myr) were not sourced.
- Carbonate porosity-depth trends were not retrieved.

## 3. Global distribution: basin types, source-rock ages, productive fraction

### Takeaway
Most oil is in passive margins and in rift/sag basins, with continental-collision forelands third. The Arabian Plate (Zagros foreland plus salt-structured platform) is the extreme case. Six source-rock intervals covering about one-third of Phanerozoic time supplied more than 90% of reserves, with the mid-Cretaceous and Late Jurassic dominating (about 54% together). Most petroleum is geologically young: about 70% was generated since the Coniacian (about 89 Ma). Only about a quarter of sedimentary basins are productive.

### Cited Findings
- Klemme & Ulmishek (1991, AAPG Bull.; USGS Bull. 1931): six stratigraphic intervals spanning one-third of Phanerozoic time provided more than 90% of discovered original reserves:

| Interval | Share of reserves |
|---|---|
| Silurian | 9% |
| Upper Devonian-Tournaisian | 8% |
| Pennsylvanian-Lower Permian | 8% |
| Upper Jurassic | 25% |
| Middle Cretaceous | 29% |
| Oligocene-Miocene | 12.5% |

  Almost 70% of reserves were generated since the Coniacian, and nearly 50% generated and trapped since the Oligocene — [Search and Discovery summary](https://searchanddiscovery.com/documents/Animator/klemme2.htm); [USGS Bulletin 1931](https://pubs.usgs.gov/bul/1931/report.pdf)
- Giant fields by tectonic setting, Mann et al. 2003 (AAPG Memoir 78), giants discovered before 2000: passive margins 34.66%, continental rifts and overlying sags 30.90%, continental collision margins 19.73% — [Wikipedia: Giant oil and gas fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_oil_and_gas_fields) (secondary).
- Earlier versions of the same classification differ:
  - 2001, 595 giants: rifts/sags 44%, collisions 24%, passive margins 17% — [Mann et al. 2001 abstract](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2001/annual/abstracts/0486.htm)
  - 2004, 877 giants: passive margins 304, rifts 271, collisions 173 — [HGS](https://www.hgs.org/node/3809)
  - Later, 945 giants: passive margins 342 (36%), rifts 283 (30%), collisions 179 (19%), plus minor arc, strike-slip, and subduction settings — [Mann 2006 abstract](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/documents/2006/06088houston_abs/abstracts/mann03.htm)
- Deep accumulations (below about 4.6 km, outside the US Lower 48): 87 of 349 petroliferous basins have them. The Gulf of Mexico, Arabian, and East Venezuela basins hold 48.6% of deep reserves. Passive margins hold 47.7% and foreland basins 46.4% — [AAPG 2014 abstract](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2014/90189ace/abstracts/1838796.html); [OGJ](https://www.ogj.com/exploration-development/reserves/article/17240568/more-attention-recommended-for-global-deep-reservoirs)
- More than 600 basins and sub-basins are known. About a quarter by number, and about half by area and volume, have production (1980 data) — [Journal of Petroleum Geology 1980](https://archives.datapages.com/data/jpg/V003/N002/187_207.HTM)
- The Zagros fold-thrust belt holds about 49% of established reserves in fold-thrust belts and about 7% of all global reserves — [Wikipedia: Zagros FTB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagros_fold_and_thrust_belt) (secondary).
- Coal by age (repeated from Section 1): Permian about 29%, Carboniferous 20%, Tertiary 21%, Jurassic 15%, Cretaceous 15% — [AAPG Wiki](https://wiki.aapg.org/Special:MobileDiff/15349)

### Inferences
- **Source-rock age pattern.** The Jurassic-Cretaceous peak reflects warm greenhouse climate, high sea level that flooded continents into restricted epeiric seas, ocean anoxic events, and Pangea breakup creating many young rifts and passive margins. It also reflects preservation bias: younger accumulations are less likely to have been lost (Klemme's 70% since Coniacian). A game should therefore:
  - (a) raise source-rock deposition probability during simulated high sea level, warm climate, and active rifting, rather than hard-coding Earth ages;
  - (b) apply loss processes to old accumulations so that young charge dominates: seal leakage, erosion breach, overmature cracking, and fault reactivation;
  - (c) keep marine Type II source rocks only after marine plankton appears in the life timeline, and Type III only after land plants.
- **Basin-type prior for charge probability (design suggestion).** Highest for salt-bearing passive margins and rift-sag basins, and for collision forelands with a salt-structured platform (Arabian type). Medium for intracratonic sags with Silurian or Devonian anoxic shales. Lowest for forearc and trench, strike-slip (small but rich), and old orogens (overmature).
- **Productive fraction.** About 25% of basins (by number) should hold any commercial-size petroleum. Within those, a few "super-basins" should hold most of it (top 3 hold about half of deep reserves).

### Gaps
- No primary 2020s USGS or industry table of reserves by basin type was retrieved. The Mann et al. numbers vary by version, and the 2003 percentages are second-hand.
- No sourced number for the share of world reserves in salt basins specifically. Salt traps are usually lumped into "structural".
- No sourced global coal-basin-by-tectonic-setting statistics.

## 4. Practical simplified algorithms for a fast game simulation

### Takeaway
Use 1D per-cell burial histories (already in the mod) to get a temperature-time path for each layer. Then integrate maturity with either the Lopatin/Waples TTI (rate doubles per 10 °C; cheapest, and adequate at Myr time steps) or a peak-temperature Ro rule (time-independent, simplest). Convert to generation fractions, accumulate charge per cell, and route it with a map-based fill-spill (flow-path or invasion-percolation-lite) on the top-reservoir structure surface. Traps fill to their spill point and pass the excess to the next trap updip.

### Cited Findings
- TTI = Σ Δt(Myr) × 2^((T_interval - 105)/10). Each 10 °C interval doubles the rate, and the 100-110 °C interval has factor 1 — [Nankai TTI table](https://geochemicaltransactions.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12932-014-0015-8/tables/3)
- TTI thresholds: 15 oil onset, 75 peak, 160 end of oil generation, about 1,500 wet-gas limit, above 65,000 dry-gas limit — [IPA](https://www.ipa.or.id/en/publications/a-hydrocarbon-generation-analysis-in-northwest-java-basin-using-lopatin-s-method); [GSC OF 1178](https://download-telecharger.services.geo.ca/pub/nrcan_rncan/publications/STPublications_PublicationsST/130/130008/of_1178.pdf)
- One critic of EASY%Ro's multi-energy claims reports that single-energy TTI-type methods can reproduce multi-activation-energy Ro results. This supports using cheap methods — [GEO ExPro discussion](https://geoexpro.com/a-cool-vitrinite-reflectance-model/)
- Peak-temperature (time-independent) Ro is a valid simplification under the Barker & Pawlewicz view — [AAPG Explorer](https://www.aapg.org/news-and-media/details/explorer/articleid/60439/modernizing-vitrinite-reflectance-models-for-paleothermal-history-calibration)
- Secondary-migration methods in basin models — [IFP Hedberg abstract](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2009/hedberg/abstracts/short/pegaz.htm); [Permedia paper](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/pdf/2002/hedberg_australia/images/ndx_boettcher.pdf):
  - Darcy flow: continuous, but slow in permeable carriers.
  - Invasion percolation: instantaneous, driven by buoyancy and capillary pressure, and efficient. Capillary entry pressure and permeability are the least constrained inputs.
  - Ray tracing (flow-path): fast, routes along the top of the carrier bed.
- Fill-spill logic in invasion-percolation code: a trap fills until the weakest seal point (breakthrough) or the spill point. The overflow then migrates to another trap, refills a previously filled trap, or leaves the model as a migration loss — [USPTO patent 10570706](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10570706)
- Sandstone porosity-depth: IGV about 40% at the surface, about 28% at 1.5 km, about 26% floor. Additional cement loss below about 2 km / 75 °C — [Szabo & Paxton](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/1991/annual/abstracts/0678b.htm); [NTNU](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2440553?show=full)

### Inferences (design proposal; numbers are suggestions unless cited above)

**A. Per-layer maturity (each time step, each cell, each organic-rich layer)**
```
T_layer = T_surface(lat, climate) + geotherm(cell, t) * burial_depth(layer, t)
TTI_layer += dt_Myr * 2^((T_layer - 105) / 10)
Tmax_layer = max(Tmax_layer, T_layer)
```
- Approximate Ro from TTI (fitted by me to the 15→0.65%, 75→1.0%, 160→1.3% correspondence commonly quoted for Waples 1980; unverified): Ro ≈ 0.65 × (TTI/15)^0.29. This is valid only for TTI from about 3 to about 500. Above that, cap the curve, or switch to the Barker-Pawlewicz Tmax formula from Section 1.
- Alternatively, skip TTI and set Ro from Tmax alone. This is time-independent, simplest, and adequate for coal rank.

**B. Generation**
- Transformation ratio TR in [0, 1] for oil, as a sigmoid of TTI: about 0 below 15, about 0.5 near 75, about 1 near 160.
- Oil generated = TOC × HI × thickness × area × ΔTR.
- After TTI 160 (Ro about 1.3%), convert retained and accumulated oil in that layer to gas, and stop oil generation.
- Above about 1,500, only dry gas survives. Above about 65,000, the layer is spent.
- Type III layers generate mostly gas, starting near Ro 0.8%.
- Expel a fixed fraction (suggest 50-80%, unverified). Keep the rest as "shale oil/gas" (unconventional).

**C. Migration and trapping (map-based fill-spill, per carrier/reservoir horizon)**
1. Build the top-reservoir depth surface on the cell grid, using burial depth plus fold and structure data.
2. Flood-fill or priority-flood to find closed structural highs (traps) and their spill points (lowest saddle to a neighbour). This is the same algorithm as depression-filling in hydrology, run on the inverted surface (negative depth).
3. Route expelled petroleum from each kitchen cell updip along the steepest ascent of the surface. This is a flow-path or "ray-tracing" approach, identical to drainage routing on an inverted DEM.
4. Each trap's capacity = pore volume between crest and spill-point contour = area × closure height × net/gross × porosity × (1 - Sw). Fill the trap, then pass the overflow downstream to the next trap along the spill chain. Losses along the path: suggest losing 1-10% of the volume per cell traversed (unverified). Tune so that GAE ends up at about 1-20%.
5. Gas-oil differentiation. Gas fills first from the crest down. When a trap is full and more gas arrives, gas displaces oil out of the spill point, so oil moves further updip. This is the Gussow differential-entrapment principle (general knowledge; not sourced here). It produces gas-rich traps near the kitchen and oil-rich traps further updip.
6. Seal check. A trap is valid only if the overlying layer is a seal (shale, mudstone, evaporite) that is continuous over the cell and not faulted through. Allow a maximum column height by seal type, for example shale about 50-300 m and evaporite about 300-1,000 m (unverified; Ghawar's 400 m under anhydrite is a real example). Above that, leak vertically into the next carrier.
7. Faults: a fault cell can be a conduit (leak to shallower horizons), a seal (fault trap), or neutral. Choose by random draw, weighted by shale content.
8. Timing check. A trap only collects charge expelled after it formed. Record the trap formation step (fold age, salt-diapir age, or deposition of the pinch-out seal). Charge that arrives earlier passes through to the next trap or is lost. Implement this by running the fill-spill each time step on the current structure surface, with existing accumulations re-spilled if structure changes (tilting, inversion).

**D. Post-accumulation alteration**
- Biodegrade if reservoir T stays below 80 °C and Tmax stayed below 85 °C.
- Crack oil to gas if reservoir T exceeds about 160 °C (TTI-based, unverified).
- Breach if erosion removes the seal or exposes the reservoir. This creates a tar seep, surface asphalt or bitumen blocks, or an oil sand if the volume was large.
- Leak slowly over time (for example, an exponential loss with a time constant of 100-300 Myr; unverified) so that older systems are underrepresented, matching Klemme's 70%-since-Coniacian pattern.

**E. Cost**
All steps are O(cells × layers × steps) plus one priority-flood per horizon per step. This is fine for a coarse 16 km grid. Run migration only every N steps (for example every 5-10 Myr) and at the final state.

### Gaps
- No sourced single-activation-energy kinetic parameters (A, E) for Types I, II, and III. Calibrating them to the TTI thresholds is a reasonable substitute.
- No sourced published TTI-Ro conversion table was retrieved. The power-law fit above is my own.
- Expulsion efficiency, migration loss per distance, and seal capacity by lithology are unsourced design parameters.
- The Gussow differential-entrapment principle and the Sylta flow-path (ray-tracing) papers were not retrieved directly.

## 5. Geometry: field sizes, thickness, depth, spacing; coal basin sizes

### Takeaway
Field sizes in a basin follow a highly skewed (lognormal or fractal/Pareto) distribution. A few largest fields hold most of the resource, and most fields are small. The extreme case, Ghawar, is about 250-280 km long and 20-30 km wide, with a productive area of about 2,800 km², an 85 m thick reservoir at about 2 km depth, and a 400 m oil column. At the mod's scale (about 1 km per block), typical fields are sub-cell to a few blocks across, and only giants span several 16 km cells.

### Cited Findings
- Lognormal field-size distributions are the conventional model. The share of a basin's resource in a given field depends on the number of fields and the log-standard deviation. Observed lognormality may be an artefact of economic truncation of small fields — [USGS, Attanasi & Drew](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70012762)
- Fractal (power-law) alternative: hydrocarbon deposits fit a fractal distribution with dimension about 1.6-1.8 down to the economic truncation size. Most oil is in the largest fields — [AAPG 1991 abstract](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/1991/annual/abstracts/0668.htm)
- Using the lognormal instead of the Pareto cut assessed undiscovered oil by 16%, because the lognormal has fewer large fields — [USGS, Attanasi & Charpentier 2002](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/comparison-two-probability-distributions-used-model-sizes-undiscovered-oil-and-gas)
- In a test play, 20% of 175 fields hold about 46% of the oil. The few largest fields hold "an overwhelming amount" of the total — [USGS OF 95-646, Crovelli](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1995/0646/report.pdf)
- Ghawar dimensions:
  - Total area about 5,300 km², about 280 km long and about 30 km wide on average. Productive area about 2,800 km² — [GEO ExPro](https://geoexpro.com/the-king-of-giant-fields/)
  - Another estimate gives 280 × 19 km — [CSEG Recorder](https://csegrecorder.com/articles/view/what-you-know-about-the-ghawar-oil-field-saudi-arabia)
  - A north-trending anticline about 250 × 30 km: a drape fold over a Carboniferous basement horst, reactivated in the Late Cretaceous — [Afifi 2004](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/documents/2004/afifi01/)
  - The Arab-D reservoir is about 85 m (280 ft) thick at about 1.8-2.1 km (6,000-7,000 ft) depth — [Resilience/Energy Bulletin summary](https://www.resilience.org/stories/2005-01-04/elephant-all-elephants-history-geology-saudi-arabias-ghawar-field/)
  - The oil column is about 400 m — [CSEG Recorder](https://csegrecorder.com/articles/view/what-you-know-about-the-ghawar-oil-field-saudi-arabia)
- Coal seams: Black Warrior Pennsylvanian coals up to 3 m thick — [Pashin 2004](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2004/annual/abstracts/Pashin02.htm). Powder River Paleogene coals more than 10 m thick, splitting laterally into thinner beds — [USGS 1988](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/depositional-models-two-tertiary-coal-bearing-sequences-powder-river-basin-wyoming-usa); [USGS 2010](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/after-a-century-revised-paleogene-coal-stratigraphy-correlation-and-deposition-powder). Devonian coals only a few cm thick — [e-WV](https://wvencyclopedia.org/entries/1286)

### Inferences
- **Field-size generator.** For each productive basin, draw N traps (for example 20-200, scaled with basin area) and size them from a Pareto/fractal law with exponent about 0.6-0.8 (from a fractal dimension of about 1.6-1.8; my conversion, unverified), or from a lognormal with high variance. Then let the fill-spill fill the largest structural closures first. Structural closures at the 16 km cell scale will mostly be large anticlines, salt domes, or fault blocks. Sub-cell traps can be represented statistically: "this cell has k small fields of size s".
- **Default geometry (unverified general knowledge).** Typical conventional reservoirs are about 5-50 m net pay, 1-4 km deep, with oil columns of tens of metres to about 200 m and areas of about 1-100 km². Giants run to hundreds to thousands of km². Field spacing in mature basins is a few km to tens of km, clustered along structural trends (anticline belts, salt-dome provinces, reef trends).
- **Minecraft scale.** At 1 km per block horizontally, a 100 km² field is about 10 × 10 blocks, and Ghawar is about 280 × 20-30 blocks. The vertical scale is not specified. If the world uses a fixed vertical exaggeration, the oil column (tens to hundreds of metres) may be only a few blocks thick. A rule like "fill a reservoir layer's pore volume above the oil-water contact with oil blocks" will then produce thin, laterally wide pools. That is realistic in shape.
- **Coal basin scale.** Coal basins are typically 10²-10⁵ km² (unverified general knowledge). That is tens to hundreds of 16 km cells, so coal can be expressed as per-cell seam packages with stochastic thickness that is laterally continuous within a cell and splits or thins toward channel belts and basin margins.

### Gaps
- No sourced global average field area, pay thickness, or field spacing. The values above are general knowledge.
- No sourced coal-basin area statistics. Individual seam lateral extents (for example Herrin, Pittsburgh) were not retrieved.
- Vertical-scale mapping in the mod is unknown to this researcher.

## 6. Rules for how a game might express coal and petroleum (synthesis)

### Takeaway
Express coal as discrete seam layers inside the stratigraphic column, typed by a Tmax-based rank. Express petroleum as fluid-filled pore volume inside reservoir layers at structural highs under seals, stacked by density (gas over oil over water), with heavy oil, oil sand, and tar seeps where reservoirs are cool or exhumed, and oil shale where rich source rock is immature.

### Cited Findings
- Oil concentrates at anticline crests, then leaks through the top seal once the column is buoyant enough — [Permedia/MPATH](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/pdf/2002/hedberg_australia/images/ndx_boettcher.pdf)
- Anticlines are the dominant trap class for giant-field reserves. Salt-flank traps matter in salt basins (14% of Gulf of Mexico shelf traps) — [SEAPEX](https://archives.datapages.com/data/southeast-asia-petroleum-exploration-society/data/001/001001/44_seapex0010044.htm); [OGJ](https://ogj.com/general-interest/companies/article/17228764/trap-types-can-be-instructive-in-gulf-of-mexico-shelf-search)
- Bitumen contacts freeze in place once oil degrades, preserving palaeo-trap geometry even after the trap is destroyed — [Ranger & Pemberton](https://searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/1992/annual/abstracts/0108c.htm); [WOU case study](https://people.wou.edu/~taylors/es486_petro/case_studies/Flury_ES486_Case_Study_Canadian_Tar_Sands.pdf)
- Oil shale is immature, kerogen-rich rock with no free oil. The lacustrine Green River yields up to 100 gal/ton — [USGS OF 87-628](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1987/0628/report.pdf)

### Inferences (rule set proposal)

**Coal blocks**
- In each layer flagged "peat", store peat thickness and set coal thickness = peat thickness / compaction(rank). Choose the block by Tmax:
  - Below about 35 °C: peat (custom or TFC peat)
  - About 35-50 °C: lignite (TFC lignite)
  - About 50-80 °C: sub-bituminous (TFC lignite, or a "black lignite" variant)
  - About 80-180 °C: bituminous (TFC bituminous / vanilla coal)
  - About 180-300 °C: anthracite (custom or vanilla coal)
  - Metamorphic: graphite (TFC graphite)
- Place seams as continuous horizontal sheets following the folded layer surface. Split seams near channel-belt cells. Add a clay or mudstone underclay (seat earth) below each seam and marine shale above in cyclothem settings (general knowledge, unverified).

**Petroleum blocks, for each trap with fill volume V**
- Compute the gas-oil contact (GOC) and oil-water contact (OWC) as horizontal planes in world Y. Fill reservoir-layer blocks between the crest and the GOC with "gas" (an invisible or pressurised marker, or a custom gas block). Fill between the GOC and OWC with the oil fluid block. Leave water or brine below.
- Contacts are flat (horizontal), not parallel to the layer. This is the visual signature of a real trap. Tilted contacts can be a rare, hydrodynamic flavour; Ghawar's OWC dips by more than 200 m — [CSEG Recorder](https://csegrecorder.com/articles/view/what-you-know-about-the-ghawar-oil-field-saudi-arabia).
- Use different reservoir rock textures: sandstone (porosity from a depth trend) or carbonate and reef (a high-porosity reef mound). Oil only replaces pore-space blocks, for example every Nth block or a designated "oil-saturated sandstone" block, so the volume stays plausible.
- Salt diapirs: traps on the flanks (upturned beds sealed against salt) and in the domed cover above the crest.
- Stratigraphic traps: sandstone pinch-outs into shale updip, and reef buildups encased in shale.
- Fault traps: a reservoir juxtaposed against shale across a fault.

**Surface indicators**
- Tar seeps, asphalt lakes, or bitumen-coated sand at outcrop where a migration path or breached trap reaches the surface.
- Gas seeps or "burning springs" over gas accumulations with leaky seals.
- Oil sands where a large accumulation was biodegraded at shallow depth on a foreland-basin margin.

**Oil shale**
- Rich (TOC above about 5%; unverified threshold) Type I or II layers with Ro below about 0.5-0.6% (TTI below about 15) become "oil shale" blocks. Smelting or retorting them yields oil.

**Shale oil and gas (optional)**
- Source layers inside the window that retained unexpelled hydrocarbons can be flagged as "tight" source rock with low-yield oil or gas.

**Coalbed methane**
- Bituminous or higher seams with Ro at least about 0.8% hold adsorbed methane. Mining them can release gas (a hazard mechanic, like firedamp).

### Gaps
- No sourced underclay or roof-shale rule for cyclothems was retrieved in this session.
- No sourced TOC threshold that separates "oil shale" from ordinary organic shale. The USGS Fischer-assay threshold (4 gal/ton) is yield-based, not TOC-based.
- Gameplay balance (how much oil per field in blocks) is outside the scope of the science and needs design tuning.
